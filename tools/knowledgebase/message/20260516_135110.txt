### Question 2 & 3: Keyboard Lag and IRQ Semantics

**Question 2:** *Am I mixing semantics?*
Yes, slightly. You are right that the PC/XT 8259A PIC is **edge-triggered**. However, the keyboard hardware itself (the 8255 PPI in an XT, or the 8042 controller in an AT) acts like a latch. 

When a scancode arrives, the keyboard hardware pulls the IRQ line **HIGH** and *holds it there indefinitely*. Because the 8259A is edge-triggered, it registers the interrupt exactly once on that low-to-high transition. If your emulator pushes a second key into the FIFO while the line is technically already high (or if you just pulse the IRQ instantly and rely on the PIC's internal pending bit), the second key generates no new edge, and the BIOS misses it. 

**Question 3:** *What is the right fix for the FIFO?*
**(c) Something else entirely (The Hardware-Accurate Approach).**

To perfectly handle fast typing and auto-repeat, you must emulate the interaction between the Keyboard Controller (Port 0x60), the Keyboard Control Port (Port 0x61), and the edge-triggered PIC.

Here is the exact cycle a real XT uses, and how you should model your FIFO:

1. **Key Pressed:** A scancode goes into your emulator's FIFO.
2. **Pop to Buffer:** If the emulated hardware's "IRQ line state" is currently `LOW`, you pop the scancode from the FIFO into your Port 0x60 buffer, and set your internal keyboard IRQ line to `HIGH`. 
3. **The Edge:** Changing that line from LOW to HIGH triggers the `AssertIrq(1)` to your edge-triggered 8259A.
4. **BIOS INT 9:** The BIOS reads Port 0x60.
5. **The Acknowledge (Port 0x61):** The BIOS *must* acknowledge the keyboard. In an XT, it does this by reading Port 0x61, setting Bit 7 high, writing it back, then setting Bit 7 low, and writing it back.
6. **The Reset:** When your emulator detects Port 0x61 Bit 7 going `HIGH`, you clear your internal keyboard IRQ line to `LOW`.
7. **The Next Key:** When Port 0x61 Bit 7 goes `LOW` again (the end of the ACK pulse), you check your FIFO. **If the FIFO is not empty, you pop the next byte to 0x60 and pull the IRQ line `HIGH` again.**

This generates the *new low-to-high edge* the 8259A needs, immediately firing INT 9 again for the next key in the FIFO. This completely eliminates the off-by-one bug and handles auto-repeat perfectly without needing to suppress `KeyPress` events (though using `KeyDown`/`KeyUp` strictly for scancode generation is still best practice for emulators).

### Question 1: Problem 1 - `ver` / `dir` take minutes

The 320ms-per-read delay you are seeing is almost certainly a **Floppy Motor Spin-up Delay**.

Here is what is happening: 
When you type `dir`, FreeDOS reads the root directory, but it also reads the *entire FAT* to calculate the "bytes free" at the bottom of the directory listing. A 1.44MB FAT is 9 sectors, but if DOS calculates free space cluster-by-cluster, or if it traverses subdirectories, it can generate hundreds of reads. 840 reads is ~420KB of data, which is perfectly normal for DOS calculating free space.

**Why does it take 320ms per read?**
In a real PC, floppy drives take time to physically spin up. The BIOS tracks whether the motor is currently running via the BIOS Data Area (BDA) at `0040:003F` (Motor Status) and `0040:0040` (Motor Timeout Counter). 

When DOS asks the BIOS to read a sector (INT 13h), the BIOS checks the BDA. If the motor is off, the BIOS turns on the motor bit in the Digital Output Register (Port `0x3F2`), and then **intentionally stalls for 500ms** to let the physical motor reach 300 RPM. It typically does this stall by waiting for timer ticks (which explains your ~320-500ms timing). 

If your emulator is instantly clearing the motor-on bit in Port `0x3F2`, or if DOS is aggressively turning the motor off after every single sector read to save power, the BIOS will execute that 500ms spin-up delay *on every single FDC command*. 

**Why `ver` takes so long:**
`ver` is an internal command, but FreeDOS's `COMMAND.COM` is likely searching the `PATH` (which defaults to `A:\`) looking for `VER.EXE` or `VER.COM` before it realizes it's an internal command. Alternatively, `COMMAND.COM` might be reloading its transient portion from the floppy disk. Because your floppy reads are taking 320ms each, checking the directory for an executable brings the whole system to a crawl.

**How to fix it:**
1. **Check your FDC Emulation of Port `0x3F2` (DOR):** Ensure that when the BIOS writes to `0x3F2` to turn the motor on, you keep that state alive.
2. **Check IRQ 6 (Floppy Interrupt):** Does your FDC emulation fire IRQ 6 *after* the DMA transfer completes? If your emulator finishes the read instantly but fails to fire IRQ 6 correctly, the XT BIOS will fall back to polling the FDC Main Status Register (MSR) and wait for a safety timeout (usually derived from instruction loops or timer ticks) before assuming success/failure. 
3. **Cheat the Motor Wait:** If you want floppy reads to be instantly fast, you can patch the BDA. The XT BIOS looks at `0040:0040` (Motor shutoff counter). Every INT 8 timer tick decrements this. When it hits zero, the motor is turned off. If you artificially keep `0040:003F` (Motor running) active, or patch the BIOS spin-up delay loop, the reads will happen at the speed of your emulator's DMA.

**One final note on execution speed:** 
If you increased the PIT to 200Hz and it *didn't* speed up the 320ms delay, the BIOS might be doing an instruction-counted delay loop instead of a timer-tick delay loop (e.g., `MOV CX, 0xFFFF; LOOP $`). If your emulator executes instructions faithfully but relies purely on real-time wall clocks for syncing, instruction loops will run exactly as fast as your host CPU can churn them out. Make sure your emulator isn't inadvertently throttling raw instruction execution!
